A dental website has one job. Someone in Frisco cracks a molar on a Tuesday night, searches "dentist near me" on their phone and picks whoever makes it easiest to get an appointment. That's the whole game. It isn't the logo or the slider or the stock photo of a stranger with perfect teeth. It's whether a new patient can answer three questions in under a minute: do you take my insurance, can you fix my problem and when can I come in?

I spent nearly a decade in revenue operations before I started building websites. I lived in Salesforce, HubSpot and Power BI, tracing every lead from first touch to closed deal. When I look at a dental practice site, I see the same thing I saw in those pipelines: a funnel with leaks. Most practices don't need more traffic. They need to stop losing what they have.

Here's what I build into a dental site so searches turn into booked chairs.

## What a New Patient Is Actually Deciding

Dental care is a high-trust purchase made under low patience. A patient with a toothache wants relief now. A parent looking for a pediatric office wants to know you're good with nervous kids. Someone shopping for Invisalign is comparing several practices at once.

None of them read your mission statement.

They scan for proof and a path. Proof is insurance, reviews, real faces and a clear list of what you treat. The path is a button that books an appointment without a phone call. If either one is missing, they hit the back button and the practice down the street gets the patient. You never see that loss because it never became a lead.

## Online Booking Is the Front Desk That Never Closes

Your front desk closes at five. Your patients search at nine at night, on their lunch break and in the parking lot after a bad appointment somewhere else. If the only way to book is a phone call during business hours, you're asking them to remember you tomorrow. Most won't.

Online booking fixes that. The best setup connects directly to your practice management software so patients see real openings, pick a slot and get a confirmation right away. A "we'll call you to schedule" form beats nothing, but the delay lets the patient keep shopping.

A few rules I follow:

- Put the booking button in the header on every page, not buried on a contact page.
- Separate new patients from existing patients. They need different appointment types and different lengths.
- Offer an emergency path. A cracked tooth shouldn't wait in the same queue as a cleaning.
- Send automatic reminders by text and email to cut no-shows.

I wrote a full breakdown of reminder sequences in [online booking that cuts no-shows](/blog/online-booking-that-cuts-no-shows). Every reminder you automate is one less call your team makes by hand.

## Show the Insurance You Accept, in Plain Words

It's one of the first questions a dental office gets on the phone. Answer it on the site.

Build an insurance page that lists the carriers you're in network with by name. Then say what happens if a patient's plan isn't listed. Do you file out of network? Do you offer an in-house membership plan for patients without insurance? Do you take financing like CareCredit? Spell it out.

Link to that page from your homepage, your booking flow and every treatment page. A patient who confirms their insurance works is a patient who books. One who has to call to find out is a patient you might lose to voicemail.

## One Page Per Treatment

A single "Services" page with a bulleted list of fifteen treatments doesn't rank for anything and doesn't convince anyone. Each major treatment deserves its own page: cleanings and exams, crowns, implants, Invisalign or clear aligners, whitening, root canals, emergency care, pediatric dentistry.

Each page should answer what a patient would ask in the chair:

- What is it and who is it for?
- What does the visit feel like and how long does it take?
- Is it usually covered by insurance?
- What does recovery look like?
- How do I book this specific treatment?

This structure does double duty. Google matches a search like "dental implants Plano" to a page about dental implants, not to a list that mentions implants once. Patients who land on that page find exactly what they searched for, with a booking button that already knows which treatment they want. If you're unsure how many pages your site really needs, I cover the math in [how many pages a small business website needs](/blog/how-many-pages-does-a-small-business-website-need).

## Real Photos of Your Real Team

Stock photos are a trust leak. Patients can tell. The smiling model in the generic lab coat tells them nothing about who will have their hands in their mouth.

Hire a local photographer for a half day. Shoot your dentists, hygienists and front desk staff, plus the waiting room, treatment rooms and the outside of the building so patients recognize it when they pull in. Write a short bio for each provider that sounds like a person, not a CV. Where they went to school matters less than why they like working with anxious patients.

This is the cheapest conversion upgrade on the list. Those same photos make your Google Business Profile look active and legitimate.

## Reviews Where the Decision Happens

Patients read reviews before they book. Plenty of them read those reviews on Google before they ever reach your site, so your first priority is a steady flow of fresh Google reviews. Ask after a good visit, with a direct link sent by text. I lay out a simple system for that in [how to get more Google reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews).

On the site itself, put a few real reviews on each treatment page. A review about a painless root canal does more on the root canal page than on a generic testimonials page nobody visits. Get permission to use the patient's first name and last initial. Never edit what they said.

## Local SEO for a Dental Website With More Than One Office

If you have one office, your site needs consistent name, address and phone details everywhere, a fully built Google Business Profile and structured data that tells search engines you're a dental practice. Schema.org has a specific `Dentist` type for this. You can build the markup with my free [schema generator](/tools/schema-generator) in a few minutes.

If you have two or more offices, each one needs its own location page. Not a single page with three addresses stacked at the bottom. A practice with offices in McKinney and Southlake should have a McKinney page and a Southlake page, each with its own address, hours, phone number, map, providers who work there and parking or access notes. Each location page links to its own Google Business Profile. That's how you show up in the local map results for each city instead of only the one closest to your main office.

I go deeper on the multi-location approach in [how to rank in multiple cities](/blog/how-to-rank-in-multiple-cities). The rule that matters most: every location page has to be genuinely useful on its own. Copy-pasted pages with the city name swapped out don't help.

## Speed on a Phone

A lot of dental searches happen on a phone, often on a weak signal. A site that takes several seconds to show anything loses people before they see your booking button. Heavy video backgrounds, oversized photos and a stack of third-party chat widgets are the usual culprits.

Google measures real-world page experience through Core Web Vitals. A slow, jumpy mobile page works against you in search and in conversions. I build lean so the booking button is visible and tappable the moment the page loads. ## HIPAA-Aware Forms

This is the part most generic web builders get wrong.

A dental practice that files insurance claims electronically is a covered entity under HIPAA. Texas also has its own medical privacy law, which in some ways reaches further than the federal rules. That doesn't mean your website needs to be a fortress. It means you need to be deliberate about what your forms collect and where that data goes.

My rules for a dental site:

- **Generic contact forms collect contact details only.** Name, phone, email and a preferred time. No fields for symptoms, medications, medical history or insurance member IDs.
- **Add a note under the message box.** Something like "Please don't include health information here. We'll cover that at your visit." People will type anything into an open text box if you let them.
- **Real intake lives in a real system.** Medical history and insurance details belong in your practice management software's patient portal or a form vendor that will sign a Business Associate Agreement. A standard form plugin that emails submissions to the front desk inbox is not that.
- **Keep ad tracking off booking and intake pages.** Federal regulators have warned healthcare providers about tracking pixels sending visitor data to ad platforms. The safe move is to keep those scripts away from anything a patient fills out.

I'm not your compliance advisor, so loop in whoever handles that for your practice. But I build the site so that the easy, default behavior is the safe one.

## What I Measure After Launch

A site you can't measure is a site you can't improve. After launch I track booked appointments by source, not page views. How many came from Google search, from the map listing, from ads, from referrals? Which treatment pages produce bookings and which ones just get read? How many patients start the booking flow and drop off before they finish?

Same discipline I used in revenue operations: find the biggest leak, fix it, measure again. If you want the full playbook on capturing leads without friction, my post on [lead capture forms that convert](/blog/lead-capture-forms-that-convert) applies to dental intake too.

## Where to Start

If your current dental website is missing more than two items on this list, fix booking first, then insurance, then treatment pages. Those three answer the questions that decide whether a patient books. Photos, reviews, location pages and speed come next.
