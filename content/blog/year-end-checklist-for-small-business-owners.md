December is when small business owners in Dallas-Fort Worth find out how organized they really were all year. The holiday rush hits, half your customers go quiet until January and your CPA starts sending emails with the word "deadline" in them. A good year-end checklist turns that scramble into a few focused afternoons. I spent nearly a decade in revenue operations, closing out quarters in Salesforce and HubSpot, building Power BI reports that leadership actually trusted. The lesson that stuck: the close is only as clean as the data you kept all year. You can't fix twelve months in one week. You can fix the parts that cost you money, though.

Here's the list I'd work through in the last two weeks of December. It's built for owners running a shop, a crew or a small office, not a finance department.

## Start Your Year-End Checklist With the Money You're Owed

Open receivables come first because they're the only item on this list that puts cash in your account this month.

Pull every unpaid invoice and sort it by age. Anything past 30 days gets a personal follow-up this week, not an automated reminder. A short email or a phone call works better than a third copy of the same PDF. People clear their desks before the holidays. Give them a reason to clear yours.

Then look at why those invoices are late. It's rarely a customer who refuses to pay. More often it's a vague invoice, a missing PO number or no due date at all. If you keep seeing the same problems, read my breakdown on [how to write an invoice that gets paid](/blog/how-to-write-an-invoice-that-gets-paid) and fix the template before January. The free [invoice generator](/tools/invoice-generator) will give you a clean one in a few minutes.

### Reconcile before you close the books

Match every payment you received against the invoice it paid. Stripe, Square, Zelle, checks in a drawer: all of it. Partial payments and deposits are where the mess hides. If a customer paid half up front in October, make sure the balance is still on your list and not quietly written off in your head.

While you're in there, decide your policy on late fees for next year. If you charge them, put the terms on every invoice and in your contracts. If you don't, at least know what late payments are costing you. The [invoice late fee calculator](/tools/invoice-late-fee-calculator) shows what a reasonable fee looks like on a real balance. My post on [invoice late fees](/blog/invoice-late-fees-what-works) covers when they help and when they just annoy good customers.

## Review Margins and Pricing for Next Year

This is the step most owners skip, which is exactly why it's worth doing.

Revenue tells you how busy you were. Margin tells you whether busy was worth it. Pull your top five services or products and figure out what each one actually earned after labor, materials and overhead. Not what you quoted. What you kept.

You'll usually find one of two things. Either a service you love doing barely breaks even, or a boring job you take for granted is carrying the whole business. Both are useful to know before you set 2027 prices.

Run your numbers through the free [profit margin calculator](/tools/profit-margin-calculator). If you've been pricing with a markup and wondering why the math feels off, read [profit margin vs markup](/blog/profit-margin-vs-markup). A 50% markup is a 33% margin, not 50%. Price off the wrong one and you're short on every job. I keep [every free pricing and billing tool I built](/blog/free-pricing-tools-for-small-service-businesses) in one round-up.

### Questions to answer before you raise prices

- Did material or labor costs go up this year while your prices stayed flat?
- Which jobs ran over the estimate? Was it scope creep or a bad quote?
- Are you still discounting for customers who would pay full price?
- What would losing your lowest-margin customer actually cost you?

If the answers point to a price increase, announce it in January with a clear effective date. Most customers handle a straightforward increase fine. What they hate is surprise.

## Gather Payroll and Contractor Paperwork

I'm not a CPA and this isn't tax advice. Confirm every deadline, form and threshold below with your accountant, because the rules change and your situation is your own. What I can do is tell you what to have organized before you sit down with them, so you're not paying their hourly rate to hunt for documents.

### For employees

Make sure you have a current address for everyone on payroll, including anyone who left this year. Year-end wage forms go out early in the new year and a bad address turns into a phone call in February. Check that your payroll records match what actually went out the door: bonuses, overtime and any holiday pay you added in December.

If you run payroll by hand or verify what your payroll service produces, the [paystub calculator](/tools/paystub-calculator) is a quick sanity check on gross to net. My post on [paystub basics for small employers](/blog/paystub-basics-for-small-employers) walks through what each line means. Hourly crews should have timesheets that line up with what you paid. The [time card calculator](/tools/time-card-calculator) helps if those records are a pile of handwritten sheets.

Texas has no state income tax on wages, which simplifies things for a lot of DFW employers. You still have federal withholding and state unemployment reporting through the Texas Workforce Commission. Ask your CPA what applies to you.

### For contractors

This is where small businesses get caught. If you paid independent contractors this year, you'll likely need to send them a year-end form. The reporting threshold has also changed recently. Don't guess. Ask your accountant what applies for 2026 payments.

What you can do now:

- Pull a list of every contractor you paid this year and the total for each.
- Check that you have a signed W-9 on file for every one of them. If you don't, request it now while they're still answering email.
- Separate payments made by card or a payment app from checks and bank transfers. Your CPA will ask, because they're often reported differently.

If you don't have written agreements with your regular contractors, fix that for next year. A simple scope and payment terms document saves arguments later. My post on [service contracts every small business needs](/blog/service-contracts-every-small-business-needs) covers the basics. The free [contract generator](/tools/contract-generator) gives you a starting draft.

## Renew Domains and Audit Your Software

Nothing ruins January like your website going dark because a domain expired on a card that was cancelled in June.

Log into your domain registrar and check three things. When does each domain expire? Is auto-renew on? Is the payment card current? While you're there, confirm the account is in your name with an email address you still check. It's common for a small business domain to sit in a former employee's account or an old agency's. If that's you, read [who owns your website and domain](/blog/who-owns-your-website-and-domain) and get it moved into your control. The steps are in [how to transfer your domain safely](/blog/how-to-transfer-your-domain-safely).

### Cut the subscriptions you forgot about

Next, open your bank and card statements and list every recurring software charge. Website builder, email marketing, scheduling, CRM, design tools, the stock photo site you used once. Add them up for the year. The total usually surprises people.

For each one, ask whether you used it in the last 90 days and whether something you already pay for does the same job. Cancel what fails both questions. Annual plans renewing in January are worth checking now, before the charge hits. If your website platform is one of the bigger line items, [escaping monthly platform fees](/blog/escaping-monthly-platform-fees) runs the math on owning your site instead of renting it.

## Look at Your Website and Lead Numbers

Your website should have a report card at year-end like everything else. Most owners have never seen one.

Pull these numbers for the year, month by month if you can:

- How many leads came in through the site: form fills, calls and bookings.
- Where they came from: Google search, your Google Business Profile, referrals or ads.
- How many turned into paying customers.
- What you spent on the site and on marketing to drive traffic to it.

That last pair is the one that matters. Leads are nice. Closed revenue against what you spent is the real answer. In my revenue ops work, the reports leadership trusted were the ones that tied every source to a closed deal. Your website deserves the same treatment, even if your "dashboard" is a spreadsheet.

If you get visitors but the phone doesn't ring, that's a specific problem with specific fixes. I wrote about it in [website traffic but no leads](/blog/website-traffic-but-no-leads). If you have no idea where to find these numbers, start with [how to measure website performance](/blog/how-to-measure-website-performance). If the site is slow or broken on phones, that's a Q1 project, not something to live with for another year. And if you already ran my [holiday website checklist](/blog/holiday-website-checklist-for-local-businesses) in November, check whether those fixes moved your December numbers.

## Set Q1 Goals You'll Actually Track

Big annual goals feel great on January 2 and are forgotten by February. Set goals for the first quarter instead. Ninety days is long enough to move a number and short enough to stay honest.

Pick three, at most. Make each one a number with a date attached. "Get more customers" is a wish. "Book 15 new jobs from the website by March 31" is a goal you can check every Monday.

Good Q1 goals usually come straight from this checklist:

- Cut average days to payment on invoices.
- Raise prices on your lowest-margin service and track whether you lose anyone.
- Get every contractor under a written agreement.
- Fix whatever is keeping your website from producing leads.

Then pick one number to look at every week. Just one. The owners who make real progress aren't the ones with the fanciest systems. They're the ones who look at the same number every week and act on what it tells them.

## Get It Done Before the Holidays Eat the Week

You don't need to finish everything on this year-end checklist in one sitting. Block two or three afternoons before Christmas, work through it in order and hand your CPA a clean folder in January. That alone makes January a lot calmer.
