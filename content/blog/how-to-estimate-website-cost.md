Most owners try to estimate website cost the hard way. They call three shops, get three quotes and end up with three numbers that have nothing to do with each other. One is for a five-page template. One is for a custom build with booking and a blog. One is a monthly subscription with the build folded in. None of them is wrong. They just priced three different projects.

The fix isn't more quotes. It's a rough estimate of your own, done first, so you know what you're asking for before anyone tells you what it costs.

I spent nearly a decade in revenue operations before I started building websites. Forecasting was a big part of that job and I hit 95% forecast accuracy doing it. The habit that stuck with me is simple: a range you can explain beats a single number you can't. Website budgets work the same way.

## What Actually Moves a Website Estimate

Six inputs drive almost every website estimate. If you know where you stand on each one, you can predict most of the gap between quotes before you ever see them.

**Site type.** A one-page landing page, a business site with a handful of service pages, an online store and a web application are different products. The type sets the starting point for everything else.

**Page count.** More pages means more layout, more copy and more to test. Service businesses underestimate this one all the time. A plumber who covers eight cities and offers six services can easily need a page for each.

**Features.** Online booking, payments, a members area, a blog, a connection to your CRM. Clutch's budget guide lists features and functionality first among the factors that drive a website's cost. The feature list is where scope quietly grows.

**Design complexity.** A tuned template, a custom design and a high-end brand build each take a different amount of design time. Design also sets how much work every page and feature takes after it.

**Timeline.** Rushed work costs more. It pulls people off other projects and leaves less room for review and testing.

**Ongoing maintenance.** Hosting, updates, backups and small changes after launch. It's a separate line from the build, but it belongs in the estimate because you'll pay it for as long as the site runs.

One more driver hides in plain sight. If you're moving off a platform like Wix, Squarespace or Thryv, the move itself is work: mapping old URLs to new ones, setting up redirects and rebuilding tracking. A good estimate names that migration work as its own line instead of burying it inside "setup." ## How to Read an Estimator's Output

I built a free [website cost calculator](/tools/cost-estimator) so owners can see how these inputs move a range. Here's how to read it, using its own inputs as the worked example.

Picture a Frisco HVAC company that wants a business website with a page for each city it serves, online booking and a blog. Walk it through the tool one input at a time.

Start with **Website Type**. The estimator offers five: Landing Page, Business Website, E-Commerce Store, Web Application and Custom Platform. The HVAC company picks Business Website. The type sets the base of the range and the base of the timeline.

Next is **Number of Pages**. The first five pages are part of the base. Every page past five adds to the range. A company with a page per city plus its service pages lands well past five, so this input matters more than owners expect.

Then **Additional Features**. There are ten to choose from: Blog/News Section, Advanced Contact Forms, Payment Processing, User Authentication, Booking System, Membership Area, Advanced Analytics, Multi-language Support, Third-party API Integration and Custom Dashboard. Each one you check adds its own amount to the range and one week to the typical timeline. The HVAC company checks Booking System and Blog/News Section. Watch this section closely. It's where budgets move most.

**Design Complexity** works as a multiplier on everything above it. In this tool, Basic Template takes 20% off the range, Standard Custom Design leaves it as is, Fully Custom Design adds 30% and Premium/Luxury Design adds 60%. Those percentages are how this estimator adjusts its range. They aren't an industry rule and they aren't how I price a project.

**Project Timeline** has three settings. The estimator adds 25% to its range for Urgent and shortens the typical timeline by about 30%. Normal assumes 4 to 12 weeks. Flexible doesn't lower the number in this tool. It runs the same math as Normal.

Last is **Include Maintenance Package**. Check the box and the results add a typical monthly maintenance figure. Leave it empty and maintenance shows as not included.

The results come back in four parts:

- **Typical Market Range:** a band that runs 20% below and 20% above the midpoint.
- **Market Midpoint:** the center of that band.
- **Typical Timeline:** in weeks. It starts at 4 weeks for a landing page or business website, 8 for an online store, 12 for a web application and 16 for a custom platform, then adds a week per feature.
- **Typical Maintenance:** a monthly figure, shown only when you checked the box.

Two details make the tool more useful. Your inputs live in the page URL, so you can bookmark the exact scope or send it to a business partner. The Copy Range button copies the results as plain text you can paste into an email or a brief.

Read the output as what the tool says it is: a typical market range for planning, not a quote. It can't see your content, your existing systems or the quirks of your business. What it does show is which choices move the number. Turn booking off, hit Calculate Estimate again and watch the range drop. Switch from Fully Custom to Standard, recalculate and see what the design ambition is adding.

## What the Market Range Looks Like

An estimator gives you one view of the market. Published cost guides give you more. They disagree with each other by a wide margin, because each one draws on different shops and different project sizes.

[Clutch's website budget guide](https://clutch.co/resources/how-to-create-a-budget-for-a-new-website) says most websites built from scratch cost under $10,000. The same guide puts a basic 5 to 10 page site from a freelancer at $500 to $5,000, DIY site builders at $10 to $50 per month and larger projects such as online stores or membership portals past $100,000.

Hourly rates spread just as wide. Clutch's data puts web design companies at $100 to $149 per hour. The same guide says agency rates run from $25 to $150 per hour depending on region and expertise.

Clutch lists the smaller recurring costs too: a domain at $0 to $20 per year and hosting at $2 to $80 per month. Maintenance on top of that varies far more from site to site. I break that line down in [website maintenance costs](/blog/website-maintenance-costs-explained).

The [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/web-developers.htm) gives a reference point from the other side of the table. It reports a 2025 median pay of $99,520 per year, or $47.85 per hour, for web developers and digital designers.

That's a wage, not a billing rate. A billed rate also has to cover overhead, software, insurance and the hours nobody can bill: sales calls, project management, fixing things after launch. So a rate that sits well above the median wage isn't proof of padding. Part of that gap is the cost of running a business.

Cost still keeps some owners offline. In [Clutch's 2025 survey of 406 US small business owners](https://clutch.co/resources/state-of-small-business-websites-2025), 16% of those without a website named cost as the main reason, down from 26% in 2018.

Put it together and the honest answer to "what does a website cost" is a range too wide to use on its own. Clutch's figures alone run from a few hundred dollars to past $100,000. That's why your estimate has to start from your scope, not from somebody's average. For the tiers and line items behind those market numbers, see [what a small business website costs](/blog/small-business-website-cost-2025).

## Turn the Range Into a Brief Before You Ask for Quotes

A range only helps if every vendor prices the same project. The way to force that is a written brief. One page is enough.

Start with what the estimator already asked you:

- Site type
- The full page list by name
- Every feature you need at launch
- The design level you're after
- When you need it live
- Whether you want ongoing maintenance

Then add what no estimator can see. Who writes the copy? What do you already own, like the domain, the logo and good photos? Which tools does the site need to talk to, such as your CRM or booking software? What counts as success: calls, form fills, booked jobs?

Paste the estimator's copied range and the URL with your inputs at the bottom. Now every shop you talk to sees the same scope and the same market context.

I go deeper on writing the scope in [planning the project as a blueprint](/blog/your-website-needs-a-blueprint-how-to-plan-a-project-that-pays-off). Once you have a range, check it against [setting a website budget](/blog/how-to-budget-for-a-website-project) so you know your ceiling before the first call. And remember that [who you hire changes the number](/blog/agency-vs-freelancer-vs-diy-website-cost): the same brief will price very differently with a freelancer, an agency or a DIY builder.

Picture a McKinney landscaping company that sends one brief to a freelancer, an agency and a builder reseller. The three quotes may still differ. The difference now comes from how each one would do the work, which is a comparison the owner can actually make.

## Questions to Ask Before Anyone Quotes

Send these with your brief. The answers tell you more than the totals do.

- What counts as a page in your quote? Is every page on my list included?
- Which features are custom-built and which come from a plugin or outside service?
- Who owns the domain, the hosting account and the code when the project ends?
- What does maintenance cover? What gets billed hourly?
- What would change the price once the project starts?
- What does the timeline assume about my content, photos and how fast I give feedback?
- Who writes the copy?
