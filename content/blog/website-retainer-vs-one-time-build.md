Every quote I send ends with the same question from the owner on the other side of the table. Do I pay once and walk away, or do I sign a website retainer and pay every month? It's a fair question. I've seen both setups work well for DFW businesses and I've seen both go sideways. The difference is almost never the payment model. It's what the money actually buys, what you can prove you got for it and who holds the keys when the relationship ends.

I spent nearly a decade in revenue operations before I built websites. Recurring costs were part of the job: software seats, integrations, support plans. The rule I learned is simple. A monthly invoice with no activity behind it is a leak. It's also one of the easiest leaks to miss in a small business budget. Websites are no different.

## What a Website Retainer Should Actually Include

A website retainer is a standing agreement. You pay a set amount each month and the developer keeps your site running, secure and current. That's the theory. In practice the word "retainer" covers everything from real ongoing work to a hosting bill with a nice name.

Here is the list I'd hold any retainer up against, including mine.

### Hosting and uptime

Someone has to keep the site online. A good retainer covers the hosting account, the SSL certificate that keeps the connection on https and uptime monitoring that alerts a human when the site goes down. Ask who gets that alert. If the answer is "you'll notice when customers call," that isn't monitoring.

### Security and updates

Software ages. Frameworks, plugins and server packages all get security patches on their own schedule. On a WordPress site with a dozen plugins, that's a steady stream of updates. One bad update can take the site down. A retainer should cover applying those updates, testing that nothing broke and keeping backups you can actually restore from. Ask when the last restore test happened. A backup nobody has ever restored is a guess, not a safety net.

### Small changes

This is the part owners use most. New staff photo. Updated hours for the holidays. A seasonal promo for the HVAC rush when the Texas summer hits. A good retainer includes a defined bucket of small changes each month, with a clear line between a small change and a new project. "Swap the hero photo" is small. "Add an online booking system" is a project.

### Reporting

This is where most retainers fall apart. It's also the one I care about most. You should get a short monthly report that shows what was done and what the site did. Updates applied. Uptime. Form submissions and calls. Which pages brought in leads. If you never see a report, you're paying on faith.

In revenue ops I built Power BI dashboards that put spend next to the result it produced. No line item got a pass just because it showed up every month. A one-page monthly summary from your web provider is the small business version of the same discipline. If they can't produce one, ask why.

## When a One-Time Build Makes More Sense

A retainer isn't automatically the grown-up choice. Plenty of businesses are better off paying once.

A one-time build fits when the site is simple and stable. Think a five-page site for a solo attorney in Southlake or a contractor in McKinney whose services, prices and service area rarely change. Once it's live, there may be very little to do month to month. Paying a retainer for that is paying for a gym membership you never use.

It also fits when the site is built on a stack that doesn't need constant babysitting. I build on [Next.js instead of WordPress](/blog/why-we-build-on-nextjs-not-wordpress) partly for this reason. There's no plugin pile to patch every week, so the maintenance load drops. A static or lean site can run for long stretches with nothing more than hosting and an occasional dependency update.

And it fits when you have someone in-house who can handle small edits. If your office manager can update hours and swap photos through a simple editor, you don't need to pay a developer to do it.

The catch with a one-time build: maintenance doesn't disappear. It just becomes your job. Domain renewals, hosting renewals, security fixes and broken forms still happen. You'll pay for them on an hourly basis when they come up, usually at the worst possible time. I broke those costs down in [website maintenance costs explained](/blog/website-maintenance-costs-explained) if you want to budget for them honestly.

## The Pros and Cons Side by Side

Here's the short version I give owners who ask.

**Website retainer, pros:**
- Predictable monthly cost you can plan around
- Someone is responsible for security, backups and uptime
- Small changes happen fast because you're already a client
- Your site keeps improving instead of slowly aging

**Website retainer, cons:**
- You pay whether or not anything happens that month
- Easy to forget about once it's on autopay
- Some contracts tie ownership to staying on the plan

**One-time build, pros:**
- One cost, then you're done
- No ongoing commitment to a single provider
- Makes sense for simple sites that rarely change

**One-time build, cons:**
- Maintenance becomes your problem
- Emergency fixes cost more at hourly rates
- Sites left alone drift out of date and can slip in search

Neither column wins outright. The right call depends on how often your site needs to change and how much risk you're willing to carry yourself.

## How to Avoid Paying for Nothing

Most bad retainers aren't scams. They're just vague. Nobody defined the deliverables, so nobody can tell whether they were delivered. Fix that before you sign.

Ask for these in writing:

1. **A list of what's included every month.** Hosting, updates, backups, monitoring, a set number of small change requests.
2. **What counts as a small change.** Get an example or two. Ask what happens with requests that go past the bucket.
3. **A monthly report.** What was done and how the site performed. Not a PDF of vanity charts. Real activity.
4. **Response times.** How fast do they reply to a broken contact form versus a typo?
5. **Exit terms.** How much notice to cancel and what you get when you leave.

Then check it. Every quarter, look at the last three reports and ask a simple question. If this retainer stopped tomorrow, what would I lose? If you can't name anything, you've got your answer.

I'd also watch for retainers bundled into all-in-one platforms. Plenty of DFW owners pay a monthly fee to a marketing platform that includes a website they don't control. That's a subscription, not a retainer. It's also hard to leave. I wrote about that trap in [escaping monthly platform fees](/blog/escaping-monthly-platform-fees).

## Ownership Terms Matter Either Way

This is the part people skip. It's also the part that hurts most later.

Whether you pay once or monthly, you should own your domain, your content and your code. The domain should be registered in your name, in an account you control. The site's code and files should be handed over or kept somewhere you can access. Your analytics and Google Business Profile should be under your login, with the developer added as a user.

Some retainers quietly flip this around. The developer registers the domain under their own account. The site lives on their server with no export. Stop paying and the site goes dark, or you get a bill to "release" assets you thought were yours. Picture a shop owner in Frisco who cancels and finds out the domain login was never theirs. That's a bad week you can avoid with one email today.

Check your own setup with the steps in [who owns your website and domain](/blog/who-owns-your-website-and-domain). If you need to move a domain out of someone else's account, [transfer it safely](/blog/how-to-transfer-your-domain-safely) so your email and rankings don't break in the process.

Put ownership in the contract. One clear sentence works: the client owns the domain, content and site code with full access on request or at cancellation. ## How I Help Owners Decide

When someone asks me which way to go, I ask three questions.

How often does the site need to change? Monthly edits point toward a retainer. Once a year points toward a one-time build.

Who handles a 2 a.m. outage? If the honest answer is nobody, a retainer buys that coverage.

Is the site part of how you make money? If leads, bookings or payments run through it, then uptime, working forms and tracking are revenue, not overhead. That's when ongoing care pays for itself.
