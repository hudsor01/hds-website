If your website is costing you customers, you probably won't hear about it from the website. Most owners find out when a friend mentions the contact form doesn't work. By then it has been leaking for months. A website doesn't send you an invoice for the jobs it lost. It just goes silent. Silence is easy to blame on the season, the economy or Google.

I spent nearly a decade in revenue operations before I started building websites. In that world, Salesforce, HubSpot and Power BI made every leak visible: a stalled deal, a lead nobody touched, a stage where prospects piled up and died. Small-business websites rarely get that treatment. Nobody is watching the funnel, so nobody sees it break.

So here are five signs, each paired with a check you can run yourself this week. No agency, no audit fee. Just you, your phone and about an afternoon.

## Sign 1: Your Contact Form Sends Leads Nowhere

This is the most expensive problem on the list and the easiest to miss, because a broken form looks exactly like a working one. The visitor types their name, hits submit and sees a thank-you message. The message never reaches you.

It happens for boring reasons. Someone changed the business email address and never updated the form. The form plugin stopped getting updates. Your domain doesn't have SPF, DKIM and DMARC set up, so the notification lands in spam or gets rejected outright. Gmail and Yahoo both tightened their rules for senders in 2024. Mail from a domain missing those records is far more likely to get filtered. Your email or DNS provider can tell you which records to add.

### The check

Fill out every form on your site from your personal phone, using a personal email address. Then time how long it takes to show up wherever it is supposed to go: your inbox, your CRM, your office manager's phone. If it takes longer than a minute, or never arrives, you have found the leak.

Do it again next month. Forms don't stay fixed on their own. If you want to know what a dead form is actually costing you, [lead capture forms that convert](/blog/lead-capture-forms-that-convert) walks through the math.

The better long-term answer is to stop relying on an email notification as the only record of a lead. Push every submission straight into a CRM or at least a shared spreadsheet, so a missed email doesn't mean a missed customer. I walk through that setup in [how to connect your website to your CRM](/blog/how-to-connect-your-website-to-your-crm).

## Sign 2: Mobile Visitors Can't Reach You in One Tap

Think about how people actually find a plumber in Plano or a med spa in Southlake. They're on a phone, often in a hurry, sometimes standing in a flooded laundry room. They don't want to read your story. They want to call.

If your phone number is an image, they can't tap it. If it sits in the footer, they have to scroll past five screens to find it. If the "Book Now" button is hidden behind a hamburger menu, a good chunk of them will hit the back button and call the next result instead.

### The check

Open your homepage on your own phone. Don't zoom. Count the taps it takes to start a phone call and the taps it takes to submit a quote request. The target for a phone call is one. A sticky call button at the bottom of the screen gets you there.

Then open Google Analytics 4 and compare your key events (form submissions, phone clicks, bookings) by device. If mobile brings in most of your traffic but desktop produces most of your leads, the mobile experience is where the money is leaking. If you don't have key events set up at all, that's its own sign. [How to measure website performance](/blog/how-to-measure-website-performance) covers the handful of conversion events worth tracking.

## Sign 3: Your Pages Are Slow on a Real Phone

Speed complaints are easy to wave off because your site probably loads fine on your office Wi-Fi. Your customers are on a cellular connection in a parking lot, on a phone that's three years old. That's the experience that counts.

Google measures this with Core Web Vitals, three numbers drawn from real Chrome users:

- **Largest Contentful Paint (LCP):** how long the main content takes to appear. Good is 2.5 seconds or less.
- **Interaction to Next Paint (INP):** how fast the page responds when someone taps. Good is 200 milliseconds or less.
- **Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS):** how much the page jumps around while loading. Good is 0.1 or less.

CLS is the easiest one to picture. That's the button that moves right as someone goes to tap it, so they tap an ad or a photo instead and give up.

### The check

Run your homepage and your most important service page through Google's PageSpeed Insights. Look at the field data section at the top first, because that is what real visitors experienced. The lab score below it is a simulation. If your site has a Google Search Console account, the Core Web Vitals report there shows which groups of pages pass and which fail.

If you want to see what a slow page might be costing you in dollars, plug your numbers into my [website performance calculator](/tools/performance-calculator). For a plain-English breakdown of what each metric means and what usually causes a bad score, read [Core Web Vitals explained for small business](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained-for-small-business).

## Sign 4: Nobody Can Tell What You Do in Five Seconds

This one feels subjective, but you can test it like anything else.

A visitor lands on your homepage. Within the first screen, without scrolling, they need three answers: what you do, where you do it and what to do next. "Excellence in Service Since 2009" answers none of those. "Water Heater Repair and Replacement in Fort Worth, Same-Day Appointments" answers all three before they've finished reading it.

The usual culprits:

- A rotating slider with five vague slogans
- A headline about your company instead of the customer's problem
- No service area anywhere above the fold
- Three or four competing buttons, so nothing stands out
- A wall of text with no sense of what matters

### The check

Hand your phone to someone who doesn't know your business. Show them the homepage for five seconds, then take it back. Ask what you sell, where you work and what they would click. If they hesitate on any of the three, your headline and your primary button need work.

Go one step further and look at your service pages. Each one should name the service, the area you cover and a single clear next step. Any page that takes ad traffic should also [match the ad that sent the visitor](/blog/landing-page-optimization-small-business). If you're unsure how to structure that button and the copy around it, [call to action best practices](/blog/call-to-action-best-practices) lays out what works on service sites.

## Sign 5: Your Site Looks Abandoned

People decide whether you're still in business faster than you'd think. A copyright year from three years ago. A "Spring Special" banner in November. A team page with someone who left. A blog whose last post is from before the pandemic. None of these break anything technically, but every one of them makes a stranger wonder if anyone will answer the phone.

The worst version is a mismatch with Google. Your website says you close at 6. Your Google Business Profile says 5. Your Yelp listing says 7. The customer has no idea which to believe, so they pick a competitor whose information lines up. Google pays attention to those details too, so mismatches can hurt how you show up in local results. [NAP consistency](/blog/nap-consistency-local-seo) is the term for it. It's worth an hour of your time.

### The check

Put your website, your Google Business Profile and your two or three biggest directory listings side by side. Compare your business name, address, phone number and hours line by line. Fix every difference. Then click through every page of your own site looking for dated promotions, old staff, dead links and stock photos that obviously aren't you.

Real photos of your crew, your work and your shop do more for trust than any badge. So do recent reviews shown on the site. More on that in [trust signals that convert visitors](/blog/trust-signals-that-convert-visitors).

## The Sign Underneath All Five

Here's what ties these together. In every case, the business owner couldn't see the problem. The form failed silently. The mobile drop-off never showed up anywhere. The slow pages never produced an error. The site just underperformed while nobody had a number to point to.

That is the revenue operations lesson I keep coming back to. When I was working in Salesforce and Power BI, the leaks I fixed were the ones I could measure. The 2,200% partner growth, the 95% forecast accuracy, the $3.7M in revenue tied to forecasting work: none of that came from guessing. It came from knowing where every lead came from and where it went.

Your website deserves the same visibility, even at small-business scale. At minimum, that means:

1. Every form submission lands in one place you check daily, not just an inbox.
2. Phone clicks, form submissions and bookings are tracked as key events in GA4. I explain how to set those up in [Google Analytics 4 for a service business](/blog/google-analytics-4-for-small-business).
3. Someone tests the forms and the mobile experience at least once a month.
4. Your business details match everywhere they appear online.

None of that requires an enterprise budget. It requires deciding that the website is part of your sales process, not a brochure you paid for once.

## Fix It or Rebuild It?

Not every one of these signs means you need a new site. A dead form or a mismatched phone number is a same-day fix. A clearer headline and a sticky call button might take an afternoon. I walk through those quick fixes, in the order I tackle them, in [my plan for raising your conversion rate](/blog/how-to-increase-website-conversion-rates-2025-guide).

But say you're failing Core Web Vitals on every page. The site can't be edited without calling a developer. The form plugin hasn't been updated in years.
