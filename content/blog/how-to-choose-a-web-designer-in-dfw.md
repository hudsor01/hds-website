Finding a web designer in Dallas-Fort Worth is easy. Finding the right one is where owners get burned. Search "web designer near me" from Frisco or Fort Worth and you get a wall of agencies, freelancers and builder resellers who all promise a beautiful site. Beautiful is the floor. What you actually need to know is who controls the site when the project ends, what it costs to keep running and whether it will make your phone ring.

I spent nearly a decade in revenue operations before I built websites. I worked inside Salesforce, HubSpot, Power BI and Workato. The habit that stuck with me is simple: judge a system by what it does after go-live. A website is a system.

I'll be upfront. Hudson Digital Solutions is one option on your list. These are the questions I'd ask if I were hiring someone. Run them on every shop you talk to. Me included.

## Start With Ownership, Not the Portfolio

Most owners open with "can I see your portfolio?" Fine question. Wrong first question.

Ask this instead: when we're done, who owns the domain, the hosting account, the code and the content?

The right answer is you. The domain should be registered in your name, under an account you log into, with your business email as the contact. The hosting account should be yours or transferable to you on request. The code and content you paid for should be yours to take somewhere else.

Plenty of owners discover the opposite on the day they try to leave. The designer registered the domain under their agency account "to make things easier." The site lives on a platform that can't export. The contract says the designer keeps the rights to the build. Now you're negotiating to get back something you already paid for.

I wrote a full walkthrough on [who owns your website and domain](/blog/who-owns-your-website-and-domain) with the exact steps to check. Do it before you sign anything new. If a designer gets defensive about this question, you have your answer.

### Ownership questions to ask

- Will the domain be registered in my name, in my account?
- Can I get admin access to hosting from day one?
- If we part ways, what exactly do I walk away with?
- Is the site built on something I can export, or am I locked in?

## What Happens After Launch

Launch day is the start of the expensive part. Software needs updates. Forms break when a third-party service changes something on its end. Hours change, staff changes, services change. Somebody has to own all of it.

Ask every web designer you interview what the first ninety days after launch look like. Good answers are specific: who you call, how fast they respond, what a monthly plan includes and what gets billed hourly. Bad answers sound like "we'll be around if you need anything." Whether you need a monthly plan at all is a separate call, covered in [when a retainer beats a one-time build](/blog/website-retainer-vs-one-time-build).

Then ask what you can change yourself. If updating your holiday hours takes a support ticket and a two-day wait, you'll stop updating your site. A stale site in a market as crowded as DFW is a slow leak. Your competitor in Plano posts this week's special while yours still shows last spring's promo.

Maintenance isn't a scam. It should be itemized, though. I broke down the real line items in [website maintenance costs explained](/blog/website-maintenance-costs-explained) so you can tell a fair retainer from padding.

## How a Web Designer Should Handle Speed and SEO

Every web designer says they do SEO. Very few can explain what they actually do.

Push for details. Ask how they handle page speed on phones, since that's where a lot of your local customers will find you. Ask whether they check Core Web Vitals, the loading, responsiveness and visual stability signals Google reports in Search Console and PageSpeed Insights. Ask whether title tags, meta descriptions and LocalBusiness schema are part of the build or an add-on.

Then test their claims. Run two or three of their recent client sites through PageSpeed Insights on mobile. It takes a couple of minutes. If their own portfolio scores poorly, yours probably will too. My [Core Web Vitals explainer](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained-for-small-business) covers what those scores mean in plain English.

Last, ask the question most owners skip: how will I know if the site is working?

A designer who thinks in revenue will talk about form tracking, call tracking and getting leads into your CRM without anyone retyping them. One who thinks in pixels will talk about fonts. Both matter. Only one of them pays your bills.

### SEO red flags

- Guaranteed first-page rankings. Nobody controls Google. Anyone promising a spot is guessing.
- An "SEO package" with no written list of deliverables.
- No mention of your Google Business Profile, which often matters as much as the site itself for local search.

## How Pricing Is Structured

Price alone tells you very little. Structure tells you a lot.

You'll run into three common models around the Metroplex:

1. **Fixed project price.** You pay a set amount for a defined scope. Clear, as long as the scope is written down.
2. **Hourly.** Fine for small fixes. Risky for a full build unless there's a cap.
3. **Monthly subscription.** Low upfront cost with an ongoing fee. Read the fine print here. Some subscriptions mean you never own the site. Stop paying and it disappears.

None of these is automatically bad. The trap is not knowing which one you're in. Ask what happens to the site if you stop paying. Ask what's included and what triggers a change order. Ask for payments tied to deliverables, not calendar dates.

When you compare quotes, line them up item by item. One quote might include copywriting, local SEO setup and CRM integration while a cheaper one covers a template and nothing else. ## Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

Some warning signs are minor. These aren't.

### Locked platforms

If the designer builds on a proprietary system you can't export from, you're renting. That can be fine if you know it going in. It's a problem when you find out three years later, you want to move and the answer is "start over." Ask directly: can I take the site with me if I leave?

### No written agreement

A handshake deal feels friendly until something goes wrong. I'm not talking about a long-term contract that traps you. I mean a project agreement that spells out scope, timeline, payment terms, ownership and what happens if either side walks away. If a web designer won't put that in writing, it tells you how they'll handle a dispute.

Need something to compare against? The free [contract generator](/tools/contract-generator) gives you a solid draft, so you know what a reasonable agreement covers before theirs lands in your inbox.

### Vague scope

"A modern, responsive website" is not a scope. A scope lists pages, features, integrations, rounds of revisions and who writes the content. Vague scope is where budgets go to die. The designer thinks you asked for five pages. You thought you asked for twelve plus online booking. Somebody eats the difference. Usually it's you.

### Other signs I'd watch for

- They can't tell you who will actually build your site.
- They push you to decide on the first call.
- They won't show live sites you can click through.
- Their own website is slow, broken or out of date.

## What a Good Fit Looks Like

The best web designer for your business asks you more questions than you ask them. They want to know how leads come in today, what happens after someone submits a form, which services make you the most money and which parts of the metro you serve. A tile contractor in McKinney and a small law office in downtown Fort Worth need very different sites. A good designer can explain why.

They also tell you no. Ask for an autoplay video slider on the homepage and a good partner pushes back, then explains what it will cost you in speed.

Look for someone who talks about results you can count. In revenue operations I used Salesforce and Power BI to hit 95% forecast accuracy. That number only exists because every step was measured. I build websites the same way. Every form, call button and booking link is something you can measure. You don't need a designer who loves dashboards as much as I do. You do need one who can tell you how they'll prove the site is earning its keep.
