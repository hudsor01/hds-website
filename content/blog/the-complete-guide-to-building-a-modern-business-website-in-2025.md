Most people who set out to build a business website start with the wrong question. They ask which template looks best. The better question is what the site has to produce and in what order the pieces need to go in so it can produce it. I spent nearly a decade in revenue operations running Salesforce, HubSpot and Power BI before I built websites for a living. Every system I worked on had the same rule: decide what you're measuring before you build anything. A website is a system. This guide walks through the build in the order I'd do it, from the domain to the first month after launch.

## Step 1: Decide the Job and the Number

Before you look at a single design, write one sentence: "This site exists to get people to ____." Call, book, request a quote, buy. Pick the one action worth the most to you.

Then pick the number that tells you whether it's working. Calls from the website per month. Quote requests per week. Online bookings. If you have an existing site, pull that number today, even if the answer is "I have no idea." That's your baseline. Without it, you'll never know whether the new site was worth the money.

This sounds like paperwork. It isn't. When I hit 95% forecast accuracy in revenue operations, it wasn't because of a clever model. It was because we agreed on what counted before anyone touched a dashboard. Your website deserves the same discipline.

## Step 2: Own the Foundation

This is the step people skip and regret. Before any design work starts, make sure you control:

- **The domain.** Registered in your name, in an account you can log into, with auto-renew on.
- **DNS.** Know where it's managed. That's where your website and email records live.
- **Hosting access.** Your own account or documented access, not a login that lives in a former contractor's inbox.
- **Business email.** On your domain, with SPF, DKIM and DMARC records set so your messages don't land in spam.

If a developer or a platform holds any of those for you, fix it first. I wrote a full walkthrough on [who owns your website and domain](/blog/who-owns-your-website-and-domain) and how to check in about ten minutes.

## Step 3: Choose How It Gets Built

You have three realistic paths:

1. **A website builder** like Wix or Squarespace. Fast to start, monthly fees forever and limited control over speed, tracking and structure.
2. **WordPress.** Flexible and widely supported, but it needs regular plugin and security updates or it slowly turns into a liability.
3. **A custom build** on a modern framework. More up-front work, full ownership and no ceiling on performance or integrations.

There's no universally right answer. A brand-new solo business testing an idea can live on a builder for a while. A service business that depends on the phone ringing usually outgrows it. I lay out the tradeoffs honestly in [custom website vs website builder](/blog/custom-website-vs-website-builder).

Whatever you choose, ask one question before you commit: can I take my content, my domain and my data with me if I leave? If the answer is no, you're renting.

## Step 4: Map Pages to How People Search

Your sitemap should mirror the searches your customers type, not your org chart.

That usually means:

- **A home page** that says what you do, where you do it and how to reach you, all on the first screen.
- **One page per core service.** "Water heater replacement" gets its own page. So does "drain cleaning." A single "Services" page that lists everything tends to rank for nothing in particular.
- **Location signals.** The cities you serve, named plainly. If you cover Frisco, Plano and McKinney, say so.
- **Proof.** Reviews, photos of real work, licenses or certifications your trade requires.
- **A contact page** that makes reaching you trivial.

Most small businesses need fewer pages than they think, but each one has to earn its place. My breakdown of [how many pages a small business website needs](/blog/how-many-pages-does-a-small-business-website-need) goes page by page.

## Step 5: Write the Words Before the Design

Design built around placeholder text always gets rebuilt. Write the copy first.

Start with the headline on your home page. Skip the slogan and use a sentence a stranger understands in five seconds: "Residential roofing repair in Fort Worth and Arlington. Free inspections. Call or book online." Then write each service page around the questions customers actually ask you on the phone. What does it cost, roughly? How long does it take? Do you handle permits? What happens if it rains?

Your sales calls are the best copywriting research you'll ever get. Every repeated question is a paragraph your site should already answer.

## Step 6: Build for the Phone and for Speed

Google uses the mobile version of your site for indexing and ranking. A lot of local searches come from someone standing in a driveway with a problem. Design for that person first.

That means a tap-to-call button on the first screen, a short form, text big enough to read without zooming and buttons big enough to hit with a thumb.

Speed is the other half. Google's Core Web Vitals give you clear targets: Largest Contentful Paint within 2.5 seconds, Interaction to Next Paint at 200 milliseconds or less and Cumulative Layout Shift at 0.1 or less. Hit those on a phone and you're ahead of most local competitors. The usual culprits on slow sites are oversized photos, unused page builder scripts and a pile of third-party widgets. [Core Web Vitals explained for small business](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained-for-small-business) covers each metric in plain language.

Build accessibility in at the same time. Readable color contrast, labeled form fields, alt text on meaningful images and a site you can use with a keyboard. Retrofitting it later costs more than doing it now.

## Step 7: Wire Up Tracking and Lead Handling

This is where a website stops being a brochure.

**Tracking.** Set up Google Analytics 4 and mark the actions that matter as key events: form submissions, phone number taps, booking completions. Add UTM tags to every link you control, including your Google Business Profile, email newsletters and social posts. Now you can see which sources send customers instead of just visitors.

**Lead handling.** A form that drops into a shared inbox is a lead waiting to go cold. Send every submission into a CRM, fire an instant confirmation to the customer and alert whoever handles new work. In my revenue operations career I wired this kind of flow together with Workato. For a small business, HubSpot's free CRM and a direct form integration often do the job. My guide on [how to connect your website to your CRM](/blog/how-to-connect-your-website-to-your-crm) walks through the options.

Keep the form short while you're at it. Name, phone, a short message. Every extra field is a reason to quit. [Lead capture forms that convert](/blog/lead-capture-forms-that-convert) explains which fields earn their spot.

## Step 8: Handle the SEO Basics

You don't need an agency to get the fundamentals right:

- A unique title tag and meta description on every page. Our [meta tag generator](/tools/meta-tag-generator) previews how they'll look in search results.
- One H1 per page that matches what the page is about.
- LocalBusiness structured data with your name, address, phone, hours and service area. The [schema generator](/tools/schema-generator) builds it for you. [LocalBusiness schema](/blog/localbusiness-schema-help-google-find-you) explains why it matters.
- An XML sitemap submitted in Google Search Console.
- Your Google Business Profile linked to the site, with the same name, address and phone number everywhere.

If you're replacing an old site, map every old URL that has traffic or backlinks to its new equivalent with a 301 redirect. Skipping this is the fastest way to lose rankings you spent years earning.

## Step 9: Launch With a Checklist

Don't launch on a Friday afternoon. Pick a weekday morning and go through this list on your own phone:

1. Submit every form and confirm the lead arrives where it should, with the customer confirmation sent.
2. Tap every phone number and confirm it rings the right line.
3. Click every link in the navigation and footer.
4. Run the home page and one service page through Google PageSpeed Insights on mobile.
5. Check that GA4 is recording your key events in real time.
6. Confirm the redirects from the old site resolve with a 301, not a 404.
7. Make sure the site isn't still blocking search engines from a staging setting.

Anything that fails gets fixed before you announce the launch.

## Step 10: Measure and Maintain

Launch is the starting line. In the first month, compare your baseline number from Step 1 to what the new site produces. Look at which pages get visits but no action and fix those first.

Then put maintenance on the calendar: software updates, backups, uptime monitoring and a quarterly look at speed and tracking. Browsers change, plugins age and forms quietly break.
