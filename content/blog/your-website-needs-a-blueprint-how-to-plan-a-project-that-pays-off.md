Most website projects don't fail because of bad code. They fail because nobody wrote down what the site was supposed to do before the build started. "We need a modern site" is a wish. If you want to plan a website project that actually pays off, you need a blueprint: a short document that defines the goal, the pages, the integrations and what "done" means. I spent nearly a decade in revenue operations. No Salesforce or HubSpot rollout I worked on started without written requirements. The ones with vague requirements were the ones that got rebuilt. Websites are no different.

This post walks through the blueprint I'd want from any business before a build. It fits on two pages. It takes a few hours. It saves weeks.

## Why the Plan Matters More Than the Build

Every vague decision in planning becomes a change request during the build. Change requests mean rework. Rework is the most expensive work in any project because you pay for the same thing twice.

A vague plan shows up later in familiar ways:

- The design gets approved, then the copy arrives and doesn't fit the layout.
- The contact form goes live, but nobody decided where the leads should go.
- The old site had pages ranking on Google. Nobody mapped redirects.
- Three people give feedback on every page and none of it agrees.

None of those are technical problems. They're planning problems that surface during the build.

## Step 1: Name the Job and Set a Baseline

Write one sentence: "This website exists to get people to ____." Call, book, request a quote, buy. One primary action.

Then write down the number that measures it and what that number is today. Calls from the website last month. Quote requests per week. Online bookings. Pull it from Google Analytics, your phone system or your CRM. If you can't find it, write "unknown." That tells you tracking is part of the scope.

In revenue operations I learned that a goal without a baseline is just an opinion. My 95% forecast accuracy came from agreeing on definitions first. The same applies here: if you don't know where you started, you can't prove the new site was worth it.

## Step 2: Describe the Customer in Their Own Words

Skip the persona with a stock photo and a made-up name. Pull real language instead:

- The questions people ask on the phone before they hire you.
- The words they use in reviews, good and bad.
- What they compare you against: a competitor, a DIY fix, doing nothing.
- Where they are when they find you. A homeowner in Southlake with a leaking water heater searches differently from an office manager in Plano comparing vendors.

Write five to ten of those questions down. Every one becomes copy on a page. That list does more for your website than any brand adjective.

## Step 3: Inventory What You Already Have

If you have an existing site, it holds value you don't want to throw away. Before anything gets redesigned, list:

- **Pages with traffic or rankings.** Google Search Console shows which pages get impressions and clicks. Those URLs need to survive or redirect.
- **Content worth keeping.** Service descriptions that still read well, FAQs, project photos.
- **Proof.** Reviews, licenses, certifications, before-and-after photos.
- **Accounts.** Domain registrar, DNS, hosting, email. Who holds the logins?

That last one matters more than people expect. If you can't name who controls your domain, sort that out first. Here's [how to check who owns your website and domain](/blog/who-owns-your-website-and-domain). If you're moving platforms, the [website migration checklist](/blog/website-migration-checklist) covers the redirect side.

## Step 4: Map the Pages and the Path

List every page the site needs and the path a visitor takes to your primary action. For most service businesses, that's a home page, one page per core service, a page or section for each area you serve, a proof page and contact.

Then trace the path. Someone searches "AC repair McKinney," lands on your AC repair page and sees what they need to call you. How many taps does it take? Where could they get lost?

Resist the urge to add pages because a competitor has them. Every page needs a reason to exist and a next step. My post on [how many pages a small business website needs](/blog/how-many-pages-does-a-small-business-website-need) goes through which ones earn their keep.

## Step 5: List the Integrations

This is the section most plans leave out. It's also where projects quietly blow past budget.

For every form, button and booking widget, answer:

- **Where does the data go?** A CRM like HubSpot, an email inbox, a spreadsheet, your scheduling software.
- **Who gets notified and how fast?**
- **What does the customer get back?** An instant confirmation text, an email, a calendar invite.
- **What gets tracked?** Form submissions, phone taps and bookings as key events in Google Analytics 4.

Also list anything else the site has to talk to: online payments, call tracking, a review request tool, your newsletter platform. An integration discovered halfway through a build costs more than one planned from the start.

## Step 6: Define What Done Means

"Done" is where most disputes happen, because nobody defined it. Write acceptance criteria up front:

- Every form submits and the lead arrives in the right place, with a confirmation sent to the customer.
- Mobile pages meet Google's Core Web Vitals targets: Largest Contentful Paint within 2.5 seconds, Interaction to Next Paint at 200 milliseconds or less and Cumulative Layout Shift at 0.1 or less.
- The site meets WCAG 2.2 AA for things like contrast, form labels and keyboard navigation.
- Key events fire in Google Analytics 4.
- Every old URL with traffic redirects to its new equivalent.
- Title tags, meta descriptions and LocalBusiness schema are in place on every page that needs them.
- The domain, hosting and analytics accounts are in your name.

Now nobody argues about whether the site is finished. You check the list.

## Step 7: Assign Owners and Deadlines

Pick one person who makes final decisions. Feedback from five people is fine. Five people who can each veto a page is how projects stall.

Then assign who writes or approves the copy, who gathers photos and reviews and who signs off on each stage. Put dates on all of it. On small business projects, the most common bottleneck isn't development. It's waiting on content from the business. A deadline for your side of the work keeps the whole project moving.

## Step 8: Split Must-Haves From Later

Go through everything on your list and sort it into two columns. Must-have for launch: the pages, forms, tracking and integrations tied directly to the primary action. Later: the blog, the resources section, the second booking flow, the customer portal.

Launching a tight site that works beats launching a sprawling one months late. Phase two goes better anyway, because by then you have real data on what visitors actually do.

This is also where budget enters. With a scoped list, you can price it honestly. The [website cost estimator](/tools/cost-estimator) gives you a starting range for your scope.

## What the Finished Blueprint Looks Like

Pull it together into one document with these headings:

1. **Goal:** the primary action and the baseline number.
2. **Customer:** the questions they ask, in their words.
3. **Inventory:** pages to keep, content to reuse, accounts and who controls them.
4. **Sitemap and path:** every page and how a visitor reaches the primary action.
5. **Integrations:** where every form and button sends data, who gets notified, what's tracked.
6. **Definition of done:** the acceptance checklist.
7. **Owners and dates:** who decides, who supplies content and when.
8. **Phases:** launch scope versus later.

Send the same document to every developer you're considering. Now the quotes compare the same scope instead of three different guesses. The gaps in a quote become obvious.
