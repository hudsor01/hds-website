Free pricing tools cover more of the money side of a service business than most owners expect. A lot of quoting and invoicing software is sold as a monthly subscription, which makes sense for a sales team with a dozen reps and an approval chain. A small service business with one or two crews doesn't need that to price a job, put the price in writing, bill it and chase a late payer.

So I built free tools for those four jobs. There's no account to create and no signup. For the proposal, invoice and contract generators, drafts save in your browser when you hit Save Draft, so you can close the tab and pick up where you left off.

I spent nearly a decade in revenue operations. A lot of that time went into forecasting inside Salesforce and HubSpot. Those platforms earn their fees when a team needs quote templates, discount approvals and pipeline reports. A plumber quoting a water heater swap doesn't need any of that. What that owner needs is the math done right and a clean document the customer can say yes to.

Here's what each tool does, which job it fits and where it stops.

## Price the Job for Margin First

Most pricing mistakes happen before anyone opens a proposal. The number was wrong the moment it was typed, usually because it was cost plus a markup that felt right.

The [profit margin calculator](/tools/profit-margin-calculator) takes your cost and your selling price. It returns the profit, the gross margin and the markup side by side. Margin is profit divided by the price. Markup is profit divided by the cost. They are different numbers for the same job, which is exactly why they get mixed up. A 50% markup works out to a 33% margin. If you think that pricing gets you a 50% margin, you're short on every job.

The reverse mode is the part I'd use most. Enter your cost and the margin you want. The calculator returns the selling price that hits it. The math is cost divided by one minus the margin, so a 40% target means dividing your cost by 0.6, not adding 40% on top. The target has to be under 100%, because a 100% margin would mean the job cost you nothing.

That only works if the cost number is honest. For most service businesses labor is the biggest line in it.

The [time card calculator](/tools/time-card-calculator) adds up clock-in and clock-out times by day, subtracts breaks and handles overnight shifts. Hours past the overtime threshold you set (40 by default) get paid at 1.5x. Add an hourly rate and it shows gross pay, including the overtime portion.

Run a typical week for one crew member through it. Now you know what those hours really cost once overtime shows up. Keep in mind it's gross pay only. Payroll taxes, workers' comp and any benefits sit on top of it, so add those before you call it your labor cost.

If margin and markup still blur together, I wrote a full breakdown of [profit margin vs markup](/blog/profit-margin-vs-markup) that walks through why the difference costs real money.

## Put the Price in Writing

A price you only said out loud is a negotiation that never ends. Once the number is right, write it down.

The [proposal generator](/tools/proposal-generator) is the closest thing on my site to an estimate generator for your own jobs. You fill in your company details, the client, a project name, a proposal date and a valid-until date, which defaults to 30 days out. Then the body: a project overview, scope items, milestones with a phase, description and duration for each, pricing lines that keep a running total, payment terms and your terms and conditions. Download it as a PDF and send it to the customer yourself.

One thing to do before you use it. The business fields start empty, so fill in your own name and details, then hit Save Draft so they're still there the next time you open it in that browser.

Scope items do the heavy lifting. Every line you write there is a line you won't argue about later. "Replace water heater" invites a fight over who hauls the old tank away. "Remove and dispose of the existing 50-gallon tank" doesn't.

For bigger jobs, a proposal isn't enough. The [contract generator](/tools/contract-generator) has three templates: a Service Agreement, a mutual Non-Disclosure Agreement and a Freelance Contract. You fill in both parties, an effective date and an optional end date. The service and freelance templates add the scope of work, payment amount, payment terms, timeline and an optional late payment interest rate. The NDA asks for the purpose of the disclosure instead. Any template takes optional additional terms. Every clause shows on the page before you download, so you read exactly what you're signing. Save a draft or download the PDF when you're done.

The page says it plainly and so will I. These templates are not legal advice. Have an attorney in your state review any contract before you sign it.

If you're not sure which agreements you should have on file, start with [the service contracts every small business needs](/blog/service-contracts-every-small-business-needs).

## Bill It and Chase It

The [invoice generator](/tools/invoice-generator) gives each invoice a number in the INV-YYMM-### format, sets today as the invoice date and sets the due date from the payment terms. Terms default to Net 30, with Due on Receipt, Net 15, Net 45 and Net 60 in the list. Change the terms and the due date follows. Each line item multiplies quantity by rate and rounds to the cent, so the lines always add up to the total. Add a tax rate percentage if you collect sales tax, put any notes or terms at the bottom and download the PDF. Like the proposal generator, the business fields start empty, so fill in yours and save the draft.

What gets an invoice paid is mostly what's on it: a clear number, a due date and line items the customer recognizes. I covered the details in [writing an invoice that gets paid](/blog/how-to-write-an-invoice-that-gets-paid).

When a customer blows past the due date, the [invoice late fee calculator](/tools/invoice-late-fee-calculator) works out what's owed. Pick a flat one-time fee or a percentage of the invoice per day, week or month. Enter the invoice amount, the days overdue and any grace period. It returns the late fee and the total now owed. The percentage option is simple interest prorated over the chargeable days. A month counts as 30 days.

Before you charge anything, check two things: what your contract says and what your state allows. The calculator does arithmetic. It doesn't know your terms or the rules where you work. For setting a policy customers will actually respect, read about [late fees that actually work](/blog/invoice-late-fees-what-works).

None of these tools sends anything for you. You email the PDF, you track the due date and you follow up. If that's the part eating your week, [automating invoicing and payment reminders](/blog/automate-invoicing-and-payment-reminders) is the next step. That's also where free tools stop.

## Budgeting Your Own Website

One tool on the list prices something different. The [website cost estimator](/tools/cost-estimator) is for budgeting a website project, not for pricing your own jobs.

You pick a site type, the number of pages, any features, a design level and a timeline. You can also tick a box for a maintenance package. It returns a typical market range, a midpoint, a typical timeline and a monthly maintenance figure when that box is checked.

It's a planning range, not a quote. If you want to know what moves that range before you talk to anyone, read [how to estimate website cost](/blog/how-to-estimate-website-cost).

## What These Tools Will Not Do

I'd rather you hear the limits from me than find them halfway through a busy month.

- **No e-signatures.** The proposal and contract PDFs get signed the old way, or you run them through a signing service you already use.
- **No sending or open tracking.** You email every document yourself. Nothing tells you whether the customer opened it.
- **No payment links.** The invoice says what's owed. It doesn't collect it.
- **No reminders.** A late invoice stays late until you follow up.
- **No accounting sync.** Nothing flows into QuickBooks or any other ledger, so you record the invoice there yourself.
- **Per-sale margin math only.** The margin calculator doesn't spread rent, trucks, insurance or office time across your jobs. Fold overhead into your cost before you run the numbers.
- **Not legal advice.** The contract templates and the late fee math are starting points. Your attorney and your state's rules have the final word.
- **Drafts live in one browser.** Clear your browser data or switch computers and the saved draft is gone. Keep the PDFs.

That list isn't a knock on the tools. It marks the line where free stops making sense. Once you're sending dozens of invoices a month, chasing late payers by hand and retyping totals into your books, paid software earns its fee. Buy it then. Until then, free tools get the math and the paperwork right.
